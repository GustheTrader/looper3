LEAPS income and VIX spike trading: risk and capital efficiency

Can deep-in-the-money LEAPS plus monthly call sales beat owning the market, and can volatility spikes be traded profitably? We test both on 30 years of free data, net of costs, then work out how to split an account between them.

1. Calibrating the option model

Free historical option quotes don't exist, so every option here is priced with Black-Scholes (or Black-76 for VIX options) on a volatility surface built from VIX, VIX3M and VVIX. Before trusting it, we fitted its settings to two real, tradable records.

  • Option price level: at-the-money 1-month volatility = 0.82 × VIX. With that setting, a simulated monthly S&P put-selling strategy earns 8.83%/yr, against 8.82%/yr for CBOE's real ^PUT index.
  • Term structure: volatility drifts back toward its long-run level at a rate fitted to VIX3M. 18-month LEAPS come out at 15.5% volatility when VIX is 12, 17% at VIX 20 and 22% at VIX 45, which is realistic for SPY.
  • VIX futures: the modelled 30-day futures position tracks the real VIXY fund with 0.95 daily correlation and the same decay (−64.6%/yr vs −65.4%/yr).

2. The LEAPS income engine

Each month end: hold a 0.80-delta LEAPS call about 1.5 years out, sized so its market exposure equals owning the shares. Roll it with 6 months left or if its delta leaves 0.60–0.97. Then sell one 0.25-delta 1-month call per LEAPS. All cash not spent on LEAPS earns T-bill interest, and nothing is borrowed.

What this means

  • Capital efficiency is the real edge. About 28 cents in LEAPS does the work of $1 of stock. The other 72 cents earns T-bill interest or funds the VIX sleeve below, and the most the LEAPS can lose in a crash is the premium paid.
  • Call income lowers risk; it doesn't add return. Selling calls brings in about 0.75% of the account each month. Rallies take nearly all of it back (payouts 9.4%/yr vs premium 9.2%/yr). What you keep is a smoother ride: Sharpe 0.59 → 0.71 and max drawdown −52% → −40%.
  • Keep selling through VIX spikes. Pausing call sales during spikes lowered the Sharpe from 0.71 to 0.65. Premiums are richest right after a spike, which is when selling pays best.
  • Settings: 0.80 delta beat 0.70 and 0.90. 1.5–2 years beat 1 year. A 0.25-delta short call was the middle ground: 0.15 delta kept more upside (10.3% CAGR, −48% drawdown), and 0.35 delta cut drawdown further (−40%) but gave up return (8.6%).

Why buying spikes fails: by the time a spike is visible, futures already price it in, and decay resumes as soon as fear fades. Why VIX calls disappoint: they pay hugely in crashes (about 14× and 7× a month's budget over 2008 and 2020), but they lose 83–93% of the premium on average. In the combined portfolios they never reduced the worst drawdown.

4. Splitting the account

Four building blocks, combined in 96 ways and tested April 2007 – October 2026:

  • Core: SPY LEAPS + monthly calls, at exposure λ of 0.75×, 1.0×, 1.25× or 1.5× the account.
  • Fade: the spike-fade sleeve at 0%, 10% or 20% of the account, funded from the cash the LEAPS free up.
  • Boost: while the spike-over signal is on, run the core at λ + 0.5 for the next month.
  • Tail hedge: OTM VIX calls costing 0, 0.25%, 0.5% or 1% of the account per month.

How much leverage?

Over 2007–2026 the growth rate kept rising all the way to 2× exposure, so a strict growth-maximising (Kelly) rule says go higher. That comes from one strong bull market. Over the full 30 years, 2× exposure fell 76–88% from peak (2000–2002, then 2008). Choose λ from the worst drop you would actually sit through instead:

Drawdown you can live withSetting
About 25%Conservative: λ 0.75 + fade
About 40% (like a covered call)Balanced: λ 1.0 + fade + boost
About 50% (like the stock market)Aggressive: λ 1.25 + fade + boost
More than 55%Not recommended

5. The playbook

Balanced account at a glance

~29%SPY LEAPS, 0.80 delta, 1.5–2 years, sized to 1.0× account exposure
~20%Spike-fade sleeve, held in T-bills until the signal fires
~51%T-bills or money market: collateral and cash for the boost
1 callper LEAPS each month: 1-month, 0.25 delta on SPY

Monthly checklist (last trading day)

  1. Roll LEAPS with less than 6 months left or delta outside 0.60–0.97. Resize if exposure drifted more than a third from target.
  2. Sell next month's 0.25-delta SPY call, including during spikes. Close or roll any call that is in the money.
  3. Check the spike-fade signal: VIX ≥ 25, VIX below VIX3M, and VIX ≥ 15% under its 10-day high. If it's on, short VIX futures at 0.5× the sleeve (e.g. SVXY) and raise LEAPS exposure by 0.5× for one month. Exit when VIX is below 18, after 40 days, or the moment VIX ≥ VIX3M again.
  4. Keep the momentum tilt in shares. Hold the top-2 rated sectors as ETFs, and don't sell calls on them.
  5. Don't hold long VIX funds as a standing position, and don't pay a monthly VIX-call budget.

6. Limitations and risks

  • Modelled option prices. The model matches ^PUT and VIXY on average, but single months can differ (monthly correlation with ^PUT is 0.86). Real fills and changes in skew will vary.
  • Early assignment. Short calls that are in the money can be assigned early, especially before ex-dividend dates. The LEAPS then have to be exercised or sold. The monthly model ignores this.
  • Gap risk in the fade sleeve. Short-volatility funds can lose 50–90% in a day (February 2018). The rule avoided that event, but a second spike soon after entry is its main risk. Keep the sleeve at 20% of the account or less, at 0.5× leverage, and respect the stop.
  • VIX funds decay and change. VIXY loses more than 50% a year when held, and SVXY changed its leverage in 2018. Read each fund's prospectus.
  • Short sample, picked after the fact. The VIX sleeves cover 2007–2026, which includes one financial crisis and one pandemic crash. The allocation mixes were chosen from in-sample results. The sleeve rules themselves were set from the research literature before testing.
  • Leverage and taxes. Exposure above 1.0× and short-VIX positions need margin and options approval. Taxes are not modelled.
  • Not tested together: the share-based momentum tilt and the LEAPS core were tested separately, not combined.
